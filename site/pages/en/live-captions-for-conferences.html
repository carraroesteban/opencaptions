What attendees get

Each room has its own QR code, printed on a poster at the door and shown on the screen next to the stage. Scanning it opens the room's captions in the phone's browser. People pick their language and read along, a few seconds behind the speaker.

  • Captions in their language. The original words, or a live translation into any language you set up for the room.
  • "What did I miss?" One tap summarizes the last five minutes, or the whole session, for anyone who arrived late or stepped out.
  • Questions about the talk, answered only from what the speaker said, with quotes and timestamps.
  • Reading settings: text size, high-legibility fonts, line spacing, light and dark themes.
  • The transcript afterwards, to read, search and download.

What you get as the organizer

  • Captions on the big screen: a full-screen page for a projector or TV beside the stage, with the room's QR code.
  • An overlay for the livestream in OBS or vMix, in one or two languages.
  • One dashboard for every room: who is live, audio levels, delay, audience and cost, with alerts on your phone when a room loses its sound or a talk runs over.
  • Your agenda imported from Swapcard, Sessionize or a spreadsheet, so every transcript is named after its talk and speaker names are spelled right.
  • An event report with every talk's length, audience and cost, ready for sponsors.

How it works

  1. Connect the audio. A computer in each room takes a feed from the sound desk (or a browser tab, or a stream from OBS or vMix) and sends it to your OpenCaptions server.
  2. The AI listens and translates. A speech model transcribes the room and translates each sentence, using your glossary for names and specialist terms.
  3. Everyone follows along on their phone, the stage screen and the livestream at the same time.

The server is a single program that runs on a laptop, a mini PC or a small cloud server. A setup wizard asks for your event's name, rooms and languages, and can create a public web address for the QR codes in one click. For a step-by-step plan, see how to caption an event.

A three-day conference with four rooms and six hours of talks a day is at most 72 room-hours of speech: up to about US$200 for captions in the original language plus one translation (pauses aren't billed), and the dashboard shows the running total for every room.

Is AI captioning good enough?

For most conferences, yes: captions appear within a few seconds and read naturally. But it's not a professional human captioner. Strong accents, unusual names and people talking over each other cause mistakes. A glossary of names and terms fixes most of the spelling problems, and pinning a room's language helps when speakers mix languages.

For proceedings where every word matters (legal, medical, formal hearings), use a human captioner, or plan a human review of the transcripts. And captions don't replace sign language interpreters for people whose first language is a sign language.

Built for real event days

Conference Wi-Fi fails. If the venue loses internet, OpenCaptions can switch every room to an AI running on a laptop at the venue and switch back when the connection returns. Event mode locks the setup while you're live, every change can be undone, and volunteers get a crew sign-in that only reaches the live controls.
