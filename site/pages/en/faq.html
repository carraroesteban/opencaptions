Cost

Is OpenCaptions free?

Yes. The software is free and open source under the MIT license, with no paid plan and no limit on rooms or attendees. You pay only for the AI, if you use the cloud one.

How much does the AI cost?

With Google's Gemini, about US$0.54 per room-hour of speech, plus US$0.4–0.6 per extra language. Silence is never billed. A 40-minute talk captioned from English into Spanish costs about US$0.70. A translated voice in headphones (🎧) is optional and brings the room-hour to about US$2.2. With local mode, the AI runs on your own computer and costs nothing.

Do attendees pay or sign up?

No. They scan the room's QR code and read the captions in their phone's browser, with no app, account or payment.

Quality

How accurate are the captions?

Good enough for most talks, classes and meetings, but not as accurate as a professional human captioner. Strong accents, unusual names and crosstalk cause most mistakes. Adding speaker names and specialist terms to the glossary fixes most spelling problems. For legal or medical proceedings, plan a human review of the transcripts.

How long is the delay?

Captions in the speaker's language usually appear two to three seconds after the words are spoken, and translations three to five seconds after, once the sentence is complete. In local mode, add a few seconds.

Which languages does it support?

You choose the languages for each room from 82 (Spanish, English, Portuguese, French, German, Japanese, Arabic, Hindi, Guarani, Quechua and more) right in the dashboard, and the AI translates between them. Speakers can switch language mid-talk: every caption track follows.

Does it identify who is speaking?

Not automatically. A crew member taps the speaker's name on the dashboard (from the agenda, the host or someone in the Q&A), and from then on captions, transcripts and subtitle files say who is talking.

Beyond events

Can I use it just for myself?

Yes. Choose Just for me the first time you open the app, and OpenCaptions captions whatever you're listening to on your own computer: a video call, a class, a video (the computer's sound on Windows, a browser tab on a Mac), or the conversation around you through the microphone. It translates into your language if you like, in big text or in a small window that floats over everything. Nothing is shared with anyone.

Does it work with Zoom, Microsoft Teams or YouTube Live?

Yes. Paste the meeting's or stream's caption link (Zoom's API token, Teams' CART link, YouTube's captions ingestion URL) in the dashboard's Integrations, and the room's captions, translated if you like, appear inside the meeting or the stream. Webhooks send them to any other system.

Running it

What do I need?

A computer to run the server (a laptop, a mini PC or a small cloud server), an audio feed from each room's sound desk, and a screen or projector if you want captions beside the stage. Attendees only need a phone with a browser. One small server handles about ten rooms.

Do I need to be technical?

No. On a Mac or Windows PC, you install Node.js, download OpenCaptions and open it. A setup wizard asks for the event's name, rooms and languages, connects the AI and creates a public web address for the QR codes. Getting clean audio from the sound desk is the part where a technician helps most.

Does it work without internet?

Yes, in local mode: speech recognition and translation run on a laptop at the venue, and phones connect over the local Wi-Fi. You can also use the cloud normally and keep the laptop as a backup that takes over if the internet drops.

Can I caption a livestream?

Yes. An overlay page adds the captions to OBS or vMix, in one or two languages, and OBS or vMix can also send the room's audio to OpenCaptions directly.

What happens during breaks, ads or music?

Captions pause, and the screens and phones say it's a break, when the talk resumes and what's next. A break starts by itself from the agenda (coffee, lunch), when vMix or OBS switches to a break scene, or with one key on the room's computer. Music between talks is recognized and not captioned, and captions come back as soon as someone speaks. Nothing is billed while paused.

Can someone fix a wrong word during the talk?

Yes. From the dashboard, a crew member corrects a caption and it changes on every screen and phone at once; the transcript keeps the fix. One click adds it to the glossary so the rest of the event gets it right, and admins can clean up any transcript after the event.

Privacy

Where does the audio go?

You run OpenCaptions on your own computer or server, and transcripts stay there. With Gemini, the room's audio is sent to Google for transcription and translation. On Google's free tier, Google may use it to improve its products, so for events with personal information use a paid key, or Vertex AI to keep it in your own Google Cloud project and region. In local mode, audio never leaves the building.

Is audio recorded?

No. Audio is never written to disk. Only the text transcripts are kept, and you decide who can read them.

Do I need to tell speakers and the audience?

Yes. Let speakers know before the event that their talk will be transcribed by AI and whether transcripts will be published, and tell the audience on the posters or the event website. The documentation has a ready-made notice in English and Spanish.

Who can change the setup?

Only people who sign in to the dashboard. Sign-ins expire, volunteers can get a crew role that only reaches the live controls, and you can add two-factor codes or sign in with your company's Google or Microsoft account. The audience pages are read-only.
